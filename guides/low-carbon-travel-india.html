Low-carbon travel in India

Guide · updated July 2026 · roughly an 8 minute read

Most advice about sustainable travel is either guilt or vagueness. This is neither. Below are the actual numbers, which choices move them, and which popular gestures do almost nothing.

Figures are grams of CO₂ equivalent per passenger-kilometre, from DEFRA/BEIS 2023 conversion factors and IPCC AR6 ranges. They are averages — occupancy and route change the real number — but the ordering is stable and that is what matters for decisions.

What this means in practice

Delhi to Jaipur is about 280 km. By domestic flight that is roughly 70 kg of CO₂e. By electric train it is about 10 kg. Same journey, same day, one seventh of the emissions — and on that particular route the train is often faster door to door once airport time is counted.

This is the pattern almost everywhere in India. The single highest-impact decision on most trips is whether you fly a route that has a decent train. Nothing else you do on the trip comes close. Not your towel choice, not your water bottle, not your accommodation.

Where flying is genuinely the right call

Some routes have no realistic alternative — Delhi to Port Blair, mainland to the far Northeast on a short trip. Flying is not a moral failure. Knowing which of your flights were avoidable is the useful part.

Things that matter less than people think

  • Reusing hotel towels. Worth doing, but it is grams against the kilograms of your transport decisions.
  • Carbon offsets. The market has repeatedly been shown to over-credit. Reducing the emission is reliable; paying someone to claim they absorbed it elsewhere often is not. Offset if you like, but after cutting, not instead.
  • "Eco" labels on hotels. Largely unregulated. A homestay with no pool and no laundry service is usually greener than a resort with a sustainability page.

Things that matter more than people think

  • Staying longer in fewer places. Three cities in ten days emits far less than six, and is a better holiday. The transport between stops is the cost, not the stops.
  • Travelling overland at night. An overnight coach or sleeper train removes both a flight and a hotel night.
  • Eating locally and seasonally. Food is roughly a quarter of a typical trip's footprint. Local vegetarian meals in India are among the lowest-carbon food on earth, and also happen to be the best thing about eating here.
  • Renting a cycle or walking in town. Most Indian old towns are more interesting on foot anyway, and you see things you would drive past.

EV travel in India, honestly

Charging infrastructure has improved sharply on major highway corridors and remains thin in hill regions. An EV road trip on the Delhi–Jaipur–Agra triangle is straightforward. An EV road trip to Spiti still requires planning around specific charge points and accepting some risk.

Electric two-wheelers are the quiet success story — for city travel they are cheap, widely rentable, and at roughly 22 g/km they are close to the cleanest motorised option available.

Avoided is not removed

Choosing a train over a flight avoids emissions that would otherwise have happened. It does not remove carbon already in the atmosphere. Both matter, but only one is honest to claim — which is why RoamWise reports avoided emissions and says so explicitly.

A realistic target

Do not aim for a zero-carbon holiday; it does not exist. Aim to know your biggest emission on each trip and to replace one of them. On a typical Indian itinerary that is a single domestic flight becoming a train, and it is often the more interesting journey.
