What things actually cost in India — and what you'll be quoted

There are two price lists in India. This is an attempt to write down the first one, so you can recognise the second.

By the RoamWise team · Almora, Uttarakhand · Updated August 2026 · Verified on the ground

Why two prices exist

It is worth understanding before you feel cheated. Most Indian street commerce has never been fixed-price. A vegetable seller quotes differently to a regular and a stranger; that is not fraud, it is how the market has always worked, and everyone participates in it.

What changes for a visitor is the size of the gap. A local paying ₹150 for an auto and a tourist paying ₹400 is not being robbed — they are being quoted an opening figure and are expected to counter. The tourist who accepts the first number has simply not played their part in the transaction.

So the useful thing is not outrage. It is knowing roughly what the first price should be, and saying it first.

The single most useful habit: use the meter or an app where one exists, and where it does not, name your price before you sit down. "Ram Jhula, ₹150?" costs nothing to say and settles the whole negotiation in three seconds.

Trains

Train fares are fixed by IRCTC and there is no tourist price — this is the one part of Indian travel where you cannot be overcharged for the ticket itself. Sleeper class for an overnight journey of 500km runs ₹300–450; 3AC ₹800–1,200; 2AC ₹1,200–1,800.

Where money does get lost is the agent who books it for you. A ₹40 IRCTC convenience fee becomes ₹400 through a station tout. Book it yourself on IRCTC or a legitimate app.

The pattern: the further you eat from other travellers, the less you pay and the better you eat. A dhaba where the trucks stop serves better dal than a café with a chalkboard menu, at a fifth of the price.

The bottled water line matters. The maximum retail price is printed on every bottle in India and charging above it is illegal. Vendors in tourist spots try anyway. Pointing at the printed price is usually enough.

Two things move these numbers hard. Season — Goa in the last week of December is triple its November rate, and Himachal in June is double its April rate. And booking channel — a homestay that charges ₹2,000 on a booking platform often charges ₹1,600 if you call them, because the platform takes 15–25%.

Worth knowing: most Indian homestays lose 15–25% of every booking to platform commission. Calling directly frequently gets you a better rate and gets them more money at the same time. Everyone wins except the platform.

The things that are genuinely fixed

Not everything is negotiable, and knowing which is which saves arguments:

  • Train and metro tickets — fixed, no exceptions.
  • Anything with an MRP printed on it — water, snacks, cold drinks. The printed price is a legal maximum.
  • Government monument entry — fixed, though there is an official and legal dual rate for Indians and foreign nationals at ASI sites.
  • Petrol and diesel — fixed daily.
  • Meter fares where the meter is used.

Hill states run roughly 20–30% above these figures, mostly because transport costs more per kilometre and everything has to be carried up. Goa in peak season runs 50–100% above.

Five specific overcharges to expect

  1. "The meter is broken." Sometimes true, usually not. Name your price or find another auto — there is always another auto.
  2. "Your hotel has closed down." A taxi driver saying this is taking you to a place that pays him commission. Call your hotel and hand the driver the phone.
  3. Bottled water above MRP. Check the printed number.
  4. Unmetered pre-paid taxi booths outside airports quoting well above the official pre-paid counter. Use the official government counter inside.
  5. "Special price for you" in a shop after a taxi drops you there uninvited. The driver is on commission and it is priced in.

None of these are dangerous. They are all recoverable by asking one more question, and none of them should shape how you feel about a country of 1.4 billion people, the overwhelming majority of whom will go out of their way to help you.

Frequently asked questions

How much cash should I carry in India?

UPI works nearly everywhere including street stalls, but carry ₹2,000–3,000 in small notes for shared autos, rural stretches and hill towns where signal fails. ₹100 and ₹200 notes are far more useful than ₹500s.

Is it rude to bargain in India?

Not in markets, with autos, or with independent shops — it is expected, and a cheerful counter-offer is normal. It is inappropriate in restaurants, fixed-price shops, hospitals and with train tickets.

Do I tip in India?

Restaurants: 5–10% if service charge is not already added. Hotel staff: ₹50–100 for help with bags. Drivers on multi-day trips: ₹200–300 a day is generous and appreciated. Nothing for autos or street food.

Why is the tourist price so much higher?

Because opening prices in an unfixed market are set by what the seller thinks you will pay, and a visitor's willingness is unknown. It is not personal, and naming your own number first removes almost all of the gap.

How we verify this. These figures come from RoamWise's ground-truth work in Uttarakhand, Himachal, Goa and Kerala during 2026, cross-checked against what our own team pays living in Almora. Prices move; where a figure is a range it is because the honest answer is a range. Where we are unsure, we leave it out rather than guess.
